9/06/2026

I find myself on the shores of Atlantic City
and I realize that after all the times
I'd been to the east coast as a kid,
I don't know if I'd ever touched
the waters of the Atlantic Ocean.

And so I do.

Despite the bleak color
that I'm sure would repulse me by daylight,
and despite the foul odor
that I'd inhale if my sinuses were clearer,
I still reach out and touch the water,
And the smell of the sand on my fingers
tells me that I shouldn't have.

It wasn't the moment of epiphany
that I had envisioned mere seconds prior.

And then I thought of Bruce Springsteen,
and how he sang a sad tale of Atlantic City,
and I tried to recall the lyrics,
as if it might help to seek insight
into another man's perspective
into this strange mirage of the anthropocene.

Springsteen begins,
"They blew up a chicken man in Philly last night
and now they blew up his house, too"
And every time I heard those words, I thought,
"What does that mean?
That's absurd.
That doesn't make any sense."

And now, as my soles slowly sink
into this sandy Sunday evening
here in central New Jersey,
I still don't understand.

I still don't know what he meant,
but I know how he *felt*.
I *get it*.

Looking out at that city, I *get it*.
I know the feeling that Bruce Springsteen had
when he, too,
decades ago,
surely stepped foot on the same beach
that my shallow footprints now inhabit
momentarily, until the next high tide.

And I see the casinos rising sharply out of the shore,
disrupting the otherwise-perfect landscape,
and the boardwalk dances gently along the seastrand
in the dark jungle-night,

And it occurs to me that Bruce Springsteen
never mentioned the beach,
or the casinos, or the boardwalk,
but focused instead on deeper,
more internal problems:

Crime.
Poverty.
Desperation.

These were the things he saw
in this pseudo-sin-city,
and these are the things
that I know I am full of.

But, here I am,
at the same place that inspired Bruce Springsteen
to have these obscure, absurd, melancholic visions
and words in his mind;

and then the tide nearly soaks my shoes
and I realize that my impression here
is temporary; fungible.

And so I squat on the beach
and I trace the sand with my index finger,
solemnly beckoning the sea to erase me,
and I step back to see my two-word poem
glowing pink and green with the skyline:

"FORGET ME".
